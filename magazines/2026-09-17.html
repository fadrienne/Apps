BBNJ is in force. The rules are still being written.

This is the governance machinery you've spent a career waiting to see built, and how the Preparatory Commission writes its financial and capacity-building rules over the next two sessions will decide whether SIDS can use this treaty or only sign it.

Story Notes

The BBNJ Agreement entered into force on 17 January 2026, four months after crossing the 60-ratification threshold on 19 September 2025. It now counts 145 signatories and 61 Parties, a faster ratification curve than either the 1994 Part XI Agreement or the 1995 Fish Stocks Agreement managed.

The real work is happening in the Preparatory Commission, which has held two sessions (April and August 2025) and a third from 23 March to 2 April 2026, drafting the rules of procedure, the financial mechanism, and the capacity-building and technology-transfer arrangements before the first Conference of the Parties convenes. Marine genetic resource benefit-sharing and high-seas MPA designation depend on those rules, not on the treaty text alone.

For SHORE and Shorelines, the story worth tracking is not ratification. It is which delegations are shaping the financial mechanism while others are absent through capacity constraints, and whether that gap narrows or hardens before COP1.

Story Article

BBNJ Is in Force. The Rules Are Still Being Written.

You have watched UNCLOS's implementing agreements move at glacial speed for most of your career, so it is worth stating plainly: BBNJ crossed 60 ratifications on 19 September 2025 and entered into force on 17 January 2026, four months later. That is fast. The 1995 Fish Stocks Agreement took years between signature and the threshold that mattered. This treaty did not.

145 signatories and 61 Parties is the headline. It is also the least interesting number in the story. The Preparatory Commission negotiating the treaty's operational architecture, financial mechanism, capacity-building and technology-transfer arrangements, environmental impact assessment procedures, has held two sessions already, in April and August 2025, and a third from 23 March to 2 April 2026. The first Conference of the Parties has not yet been scheduled.

This is the phase that decides whether BBNJ is a treaty small states can use or a treaty they merely signed. High-seas marine protected areas, mandatory environmental impact assessments for activities beyond national jurisdiction, and benefit-sharing from marine genetic resources are the headline provisions. None of them function without a financial mechanism that a state without a standing delegation in New York or Geneva can actually draw on, and without technology-transfer commitments that go beyond a line in the text.

You have sat in enough multilateral rooms to know how this part goes. The states with permanent missions and trained legal teams write the financial mechanism's fine print. The states without them show up for the plenary and absorb whatever the drafting group produced. SIDS delegations, Seychelles among them, face exactly this staffing constraint at PrepCom, not because the issue does not matter to them, but because sending the same specialist to a fourth overlapping negotiation in a year is not something most small foreign ministries can do.

The counterargument is that BBNJ's own text mandates a voluntary trust fund and a special fund for capacity building, so the financing question is already answered. It is not. A fund existing in a treaty article and a fund with contribution levels, disbursement rules, and access criteria that a small delegation can actually navigate are different things, and that gap is exactly what PrepCom is negotiating now, largely out of public view.

For Shorelines, the piece is not "BBNJ enters into force," which every outlet from UN News to the Congressional Research Service has already covered. It is what the financial mechanism looks like once PrepCom3's output becomes public, and whether it was built by the states most likely to need it or the states least likely to. That is a story you can only write once the text is out, so the move now is to flag it, not file it.

Seychelles Built the Plan. Now It Needs the Money to Run It.

You have made the High-Income SIDS Paradox argument enough times that it risks sounding abstract if it is not anchored in a live example. Here is one, in your own waters. Seychelles's Marine Spatial Plan, completed in 2024 with zoning implementation starting in 2025, is the second largest MSP process in the world by area and the first comprehensive plan anywhere in the Western Indian Ocean. Marine protected areas under it now cover more than 444,000 square kilometres, 33% of the country's total maritime jurisdiction, exceeding the 30% target the government committed to.

That is the achievement. The paradox sits in what happens next. Seychelles is classified as a high-income country, a status built on GDP per capita that does not account for the fiscal exposure of a 1.3 million square kilometre EEZ with a population under 100,000 to administer it. High-income classification routes Seychelles away from concessional finance instruments built for lower-income states, even though the administrative burden of enforcing an MSP at this scale is not smaller because the country is classified as wealthy.

The GEF-7 Blue Economy Project is the mechanism doing the unglamorous work now: building the institutional capacity to actually implement the zoning that exists on paper, and expanding two MPAs in the Inner Islands specifically. Plans and zones are not self-enforcing. They require monitoring capacity, enforcement vessels, staff trained in compliance, and a legal apparatus that can prosecute violations inside zones that exist mostly as coordinates on a chart until someone patrols them. That is precisely the capacity gap high-income classification assumes away.

The counterargument, that Seychelles chose high-income status advantages, tourism revenue, tax treaty access, that it cannot now selectively opt back into concessional finance when convenient, is worth naming directly because donors raise it. The answer is not that Seychelles wants exemption from its income classification. It is that GDP per capita was never built to measure fiscal exposure per square kilometre of ocean under national jurisdiction, and a country can be simultaneously income-rich and governance-capacity-poor relative to the size of the space it is legally responsible for.

This is the exact case study that turns the High-Income SIDS Paradox from an argument into evidence: a state that built the plan, hit the target, and still needed a GEF grant to make the plan real. That is worth a Shorelines piece on its own, built around the gap between the 444,000 square kilometre figure and what it costs to actually enforce it.

Open data and data sovereignty are not the same argument, and Seychelles needs both

The tension between open science and data sovereignty is playing out in exactly the terms your science-to-policy pipeline work depends on, and CARE Principles exceptions built into NSF's polar data policy are a template SHORE could adapt for Seychelles's own marine data.

Story Notes

A live tension is playing out in geoscience data policy: open science norms push for unrestricted data sharing, while data sovereignty, particularly around Indigenous and national data, pushes back with legitimate grounds. NSF's Office of Polar Programs now writes explicit exceptions into its open-data policy for Indigenous knowledge and data, citing sensitivity, privacy, sovereignty, and intellectual property rights as reasons to withhold rather than default to open release.

This is not an abstract academic debate. It is the exact fault line running through the science-to-policy pipeline: who gets to decide whether Seychelles's own marine and bathymetric data is shared globally through platforms like Seabed 2030, versus retained under national or community control first.

The FAIR and CARE Principles combination, Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable alongside Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics, is the specific framework doing this balancing work in the NSF policy and is worth reading directly before drafting anything for Shorelines on this.

https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53480-2

Story Article

Open Data and Data Sovereignty Are Not the Same Argument, and Seychelles Needs Both

Every conversation about ocean data sharing eventually collapses into a single axis, open versus closed, as though the only choice is how much to release. NSF's Office of Polar Programs is demonstrating a more precise framing, one directly useful for how SHORE positions Seychelles's own marine data going forward.

The policy directs funded researchers to align data management with both FAIR and CARE Principles. FAIR, Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable, is the open-science standard most funders already require. CARE, Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics, is newer, built specifically around Indigenous data governance, and it does something FAIR does not: it asks who benefits from data release and who retains authority over it, not just whether the data is technically accessible.

The policy's explicit exception is the part worth reading closely: it permits withholding data from open release "where sensitivity, privacy, sovereignty, and/or intellectual property rights might take precedence." That is not a loophole. It is a deliberate acknowledgment that open science defaults can conflict with a community's or a state's legitimate claim to control its own information before it becomes a global public good.

This maps directly onto a decision Seychelles has not yet had to make explicitly but will: as bathymetric and marine spatial data collected under the MSP process and GEF-7 Blue Economy Project accumulates, some of it will be candidate data for global platforms like Seabed 2030's crowdsourced bathymetry programme. The current default in most such platforms leans FAIR: share it, make it interoperable, let anyone build on it. What CARE adds is the question of whether Seychelles, or specific communities within it, should retain authority over how that data gets used before it is folded into a global grid that others control.

The counterargument from the open-science side is real: fragmented, sovereignty-gated ocean data slows exactly the kind of global coordination Seabed 2030 depends on to close mapping gaps, and a Western Indian Ocean data sprint, the one this edition's Seabed 2030 story argues for, would struggle if every contributing state applied a CARE-style authority-to-control clause to its contributions.

That tension does not resolve cleanly, and it should not. What NSF's policy demonstrates is that FAIR and CARE can coexist inside one funding framework rather than one defeating the other, which is closer to the negotiated position Seychelles will eventually need than an all-open or all-closed default. That is worth a full Shorelines piece on its own: what a Seychelles-specific FAIR-plus-CARE data policy for marine and bathymetric data would actually look like, built before an external platform's default terms make the decision for you.

Two Ways to Map the Same Ocean, and Why the Difference Is the Story

Seabed 2030's model is publicly stated and easy to describe: crowdsourced bathymetry, open data release, a shared global grid anyone can build on. China's expansion of large-scale ocean mapping runs on a different logic, one with acknowledged strategic and military dimensions, where the resulting data serves national security and resource-access objectives rather than open global release.

Both are expanding in the same ocean basins, sometimes literally the same waters. That is worth stating without either alarmism or false equivalence. It is not that one mapping programme is good and the other bad in the abstract; states have always had legitimate strategic reasons to survey their own approaches and adjacent waters. It is that the two models produce fundamentally different assets: one becomes a shared public good that a country like Seychelles can build governance decisions on top of, the other becomes a strategic asset held by whichever state or actor collected it.

This matters directly for how SHORE frames any Western Indian Ocean mapping proposal. "Closing the mapping gap" sounds like a single, shared objective, but coverage percentage alone does not tell you which model produced it. A basin mapped at 90% by a strategically-motivated programme is not equivalent, for a coastal state's own governance purposes, to the same basin mapped at 60% under an open, CARE-compliant, Seabed 2030-aligned model, because only the second is usable by Seychelles itself for planning, enforcement, or academic research without a data-sharing negotiation first.

The counterargument, that any additional bathymetric data improves global scientific understanding regardless of who collected it or why, has some truth to it: oceanographic and climate science does benefit from more coverage in the aggregate. But that argument elides the access question entirely. Data collected for strategic purposes is not typically released to the coastal states whose waters it covers, which means more total ocean floor being mapped globally does not automatically mean more usable data reaching Seychelles specifically.

For a Shorelines piece, this is the sharper version of the Western Indian Ocean data sprint argument already building across this edition: the pitch to Seabed 2030 is not just about closing a coverage gap, it is about ensuring the coverage that does close in this region closes under an open, nationally-accessible model before a strategically-motivated one fills the same gap on different terms.
