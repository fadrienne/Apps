0.2% of a Global Total

Share of global climate finance reaching SIDS as adaptation funding

0.2%

A new analysis putting Small Island Developing States' unmet climate finance need at roughly US$12 billion a year, against adaptation finance flows of just 0.2 per cent of the global total, is the hard number the High-Income SIDS Paradox argument has been missing.

Story Notes

A March 2026 analysis finds Small Island Developing States are being systematically locked out of climate finance because the global financing system treats them as too small and too fragmented to fund efficiently. International adaptation finance flows to SIDS sit at just 0.2 per cent of global climate finance, against an estimated need of roughly US$12 billion a year.

SIDS across the Caribbean, Pacific and Indian Oceans face some of the most severe and immediate climate impacts while contributing about 1 per cent of global carbon dioxide emissions, a mismatch the report frames as central to the exclusion, not incidental to it.

forbes.com/sites/jamiehailstone/2026/03/04/small-island-states-locked-out-of-climate-finance-study-finds
Story Article

Zero point two per cent. That is the share of global climate finance reaching Small Island Developing States as adaptation funding, against an estimated unmet need across those states of roughly US$12 billion a year, according to a March 2026 analysis. The explanation the report gives is not that SIDS projects are weak. It is that the global financing system is structurally built around scale, and a state with a small population and a small absolute budget reads as inefficient to finance regardless of how urgent or well designed the project is.

This is the number the High-Income SIDS Paradox argument has needed and not quite had. The paradox, as you have framed it, is that Seychelles carries every physical vulnerability of a small island state while its GNI per capita locks it out of concessional finance reserved for lower-income countries. That framing has always been correct directionally. It has lacked a hard figure showing how thin the finance actually is even for SIDS that clear the income bar entirely.

The 0.2 per cent figure answers a different, sharper question: even setting income classification aside, the entire category of Small Island Developing States receives a rounding error's worth of global adaptation finance. Seychelles sits inside that undersized allocation and then gets a second exclusion layered on top, the high-income classification that closes off concessional windows other SIDS can still access. The paradox is not one gate. It is two, stacked.

Name the caveat before it gets raised for you: an aggregated 0.2 per cent figure across all SIDS masks real variation between them, and it is worth confirming what exactly the underlying study counts as adaptation finance and over what period, before citing the number as a headline figure in a Shorelines piece. A number this useful is worth one afternoon spent reading the source methodology rather than the press coverage of it.

With that check done, the piece writes itself: take the 0.2 per cent global figure, lay Seychelles's high-income exclusion on top of it, and the argument that a decade of GIS and maritime boundary work has been circling finally has its anchor statistic. That is a stronger opening than another paragraph describing the paradox in the abstract.

STORY ARTICLE

Most GeoAI papers report a result: a model, a study area, an accuracy score. This one documents a workflow instead, a complete process for turning raw imagery into finished habitat and vessel-detection maps through AI model predictions, built to be repeated rather than admired once. That distinction is what makes it worth reading closely rather than filing as one more coral-mapping paper.

For the Ocean Mapping Hub, this is a blueprint question more than a technology question. Name the honest limitation: a published workflow trained and validated on one set of reef systems and imagery conditions does not transfer cleanly to Seychelles's granitic and coralline reef structures without recalibration. The realistic use is architectural, not literal. Reading this workflow closely and mapping its pipeline stages against what the Ocean Mapping Hub needs, coral segmentation for reef monitoring, vessel detection for fisheries surveillance, gives a concrete structure to evaluate build-versus-adapt decisions against, rather than starting that conversation with a blank page.

The near-term task is a working session, alone or with whoever is scoping the Hub's technical architecture, reading this paper's methodology section specifically and listing which of its three components map onto a near-term Seychelles pilot, and which would need a different approach entirely given local reef and imagery conditions.

Renaming the Small Island

A new peer-reviewed paper renaming Small Island Developing States as Big Ocean States through the blue economy lens gives the High-Income SIDS Paradox argument a live academic vocabulary to sit inside, rather than standing alone.

Story Notes

A paper published in Geo: Geography and Environment, "From 'Small Island Developing State' to 'Big Ocean State': The Blue Economy and the Improvised (Re)Scaling of an Ocean Country," examines how the blue economy framing has been used to rescale how ocean nations describe themselves, shifting the emphasis from small land area to vast exclusive economic zone.

The Big Ocean State framing originated largely in Pacific SIDS advocacy, built on the argument that a country with a tiny land footprint but a maritime zone spanning millions of square kilometres is better described by its ocean territory than its coastline.

Source: Geo: Geography and Environment, Royal Geographical Society-IBG, doi.org/10.1002/geo2.70072

Story Article

The term Big Ocean State did not emerge from an academic seminar. It came out of Pacific SIDS advocacy, coined by leaders in Kiribati, Tuvalu and the wider Pacific Islands Forum making a specific rhetorical move: reject the framing of smallness inherited from land-area geography and insist instead on being measured by exclusive economic zone. A country with a few hundred square kilometres of land and a few million square kilometres of ocean is not small by any honest reading. It has simply been described using the wrong denominator.

This paper traces that rescaling as an academic subject in its own right, examining the blue economy as the mechanism through which the reframing gets operationalised, not just declared. Calling yourself a Big Ocean State is a rhetorical claim. Building a blue economy strategy, a maritime boundary regime, an ocean governance architecture around that claim is what makes the rescaling durable rather than a slogan.

Seychelles fits this framing exactly and has rarely been discussed inside it. A land area of roughly 460 square kilometres against an exclusive economic zone of 1.4 million square kilometres is a Big Ocean State ratio more extreme than several of the Pacific states where the term originated. Yet the paper's framing, and the broader Big Ocean State literature it sits inside, is built almost entirely from Pacific case studies. The Indian Ocean, Seychelles specifically, is close to absent from this academic conversation.

Name the reason that gap probably exists rather than assuming it is oversight: Pacific SIDS built a coordinated regional advocacy architecture, the Pacific Islands Forum foremost among it, that pushed the Big Ocean State framing into international forums deliberately and repeatedly. The Indian Ocean SIDS have no equivalent coordinated advocacy body making the same rhetorical push, so the academic literature studying the framing has, understandably, followed where the advocacy was loudest.

That gap is the opening. A paper, or a substantial Shorelines piece, extending the Big Ocean State framing to Seychelles specifically, using maritime boundary delimitation experience to ground the argument in the actual EEZ geography rather than the rhetoric alone, would be filling a real absence in a live academic conversation rather than restating an established one. That is a stronger publishing angle than most topics currently in the pipeline, because the gap is specific, citable and largely untouched.

The Platform Decided Before You're Consulted

Delegates at the BBNJ Preparatory Commission's third session flagged the need for a formal consultation process on the treaty's Clearing House Mechanism before COP1, and that information architecture decision will shape whether Seychelles's hydrospatial data ends up duplicated across yet another global reporting platform.

Every clearing-house architecture decided before the consultation opens becomes a reporting obligation inherited without being consulted on.
Story Notes

The BBNJ Agreement entered into force on 17 January 2026 with 83 parties. Its third Preparatory Commission session ran 23 March to 2 April 2026, and delegates underscored the importance of establishing a formal consultation process for developing the treaty's Clearing House Mechanism between PrepCom III and COP1.

The Clearing House Mechanism is the treaty's designated information-sharing platform, the system through which parties are expected to report and access data on marine genetic resources, area-based management tools and environmental impact assessments under the high seas regime.

enb.iisd.org/marine-biodiversity-beyond-national-jurisdiction-bbnj-cop-prepcom3-26mar2026
Story Article

A Clearing House Mechanism sounds like plumbing, the unglamorous back-end system nobody outside treaty secretariats thinks about. It is also the system that determines, in practice, whether a coastal state's own hydrospatial and marine data infrastructure has to duplicate itself to satisfy a new global reporting layer, or whether it can plug into that layer using data structures it has already built. That decision has not been made yet. The BBNJ Preparatory Commission's third session, held from 23 March to 2 April 2026, flagged the need for a formal consultation process on exactly this question, to run between PrepCom III and COP1.

This matters more than it looks like it should, because information architecture decisions made early in a treaty's implementation tend to calcify. Once a Clearing House Mechanism's data standards, submission formats and access protocols are built, retrofitting them to accommodate a different national reporting structure becomes a multi-year technical negotiation rather than a design choice. Whatever gets decided in this consultation window is likely to be what parties live with for the treaty's first decade.

For Seychelles, and for SHORE Institute's own data governance thinking, that raises a direct and practical question. Does the CHM's eventual reporting format align with the kind of marine spatial data infrastructure the Ocean Mapping Hub is building, or will Seychelles's government need a separate reporting layer built specifically to satisfy BBNJ obligations, disconnected from national systems already in use.

Name the honest risk in this consultation process directly: formal UN-level consultations on technical infrastructure questions routinely open for input well after the substantive architecture has already been informally shaped by whichever secretariat staff and early-mover states did the preparatory technical work. A consultation window is real and worth engaging, but it is not the same as a blank slate.

The concrete task is tracking the Preparatory Commission's published outputs and the IISD Earth Negotiations Bulletin's ongoing PrepCom coverage for the moment this consultation process formally opens, and flagging it to whoever in Seychelles's government handles BBNJ implementation, likely within the Department of Blue Economy or Foreign Affairs, so that a national submission on data interoperability gets filed rather than missed. Given the maritime boundary delimitation background this office brings to exactly this kind of technical treaty detail, a drafted contribution here carries real weight if it lands inside the window rather than after it closes.

A Case Study Worth Submitting

The co-facilitators of the UNFCCC's 2026 Ocean and Climate Change Dialogue have opened a call for submissions on financing sub-themes and case studies, and a short submission citing Seychelles's own blue finance record, debt swap included, is a low-effort way to get SHORE Institute's name into the record before the Dialogue convenes.

Story Notes

A message from the co-facilitator of the 2026 Ocean and Climate Change Dialogue, mandated under the UNFCCC since COP25, calls for submissions on possible sub-themes for the Dialogue's programme, alongside case studies on financing of the ocean and water systems, feeding into the Standing Committee on Finance's 2026 Forum on this same topic.

The letter is addressed to Parties and Observers, meaning non-party organisations, research institutes among them, are explicitly invited to contribute rather than only national delegations.

unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/message_to_parties_and_observers_letter_2026_ocean_and_climate_change_dialogue.pdf
Story Article

The Ocean and Climate Change Dialogue has run under the UNFCCC every year since COP25, a standing forum for exactly the kind of technical, finance-adjacent ocean policy work that rarely makes headlines but shapes what the Standing Committee on Finance eventually recommends. The 2026 edition's co-facilitator has opened a call for submissions on two fronts: suggested sub-themes for the Dialogue's programme, and case studies specifically on financing the ocean and water systems.

Observers, not just party delegations, are explicitly invited to submit. That detail is easy to miss reading a formal UNFCCC letter, but it means a research institute with a documented case study in hand has a real, low-friction channel into this process, not a hypothetical one requiring government sponsorship to access.

Seychelles has exactly that case study sitting ready, and it is a stronger one than the version most people would reach for first. The instinct would be to submit the celebratory account of the 2015 debt-for-nature swap, the version cited for a decade across UNFCCC technical papers and AOSIS advocacy. Research revisiting that swap, covered in this edition's sibling piece last week, found it increased Seychelles's public indebtedness rather than reducing it, and secured little beyond protections the government had already committed to.

That complicated finding is the more valuable submission, not the weaker one. A Standing Committee on Finance forum built to scale ocean and water financing mechanisms across SIDS needs to know which flagship mechanisms actually deliver what they are sold as delivering, not another round of the case study everyone already cites approvingly. A submission that names the swap's real limitations alongside its real benefits reads as credible technical input from people who have actually examined the mechanism, not advocacy dressed as evidence.

Name the risk before submitting: a critical case study, sent to a body focused on scaling finance mechanisms, could land as unhelpful if framed as a takedown rather than a technical assessment. The framing needs to be additionality and design lessons, what worked, what did not, what a next-generation debt swap should do differently, not a case against debt swaps as a category.

The concrete task is finding the submission portal and actual deadline on the UNFCCC website this week, since the letter itself did not specify one in the material reviewed here, and drafting a two-page technical submission built around the swap's documented findings, framed as design lessons for the next mechanism rather than as criticism of the last one.

The Taxonomy You Can Cite

A review paper cataloguing the challenges and opportunities of geospatial technologies for Marine Spatial Planning gives you a ready-made literature scaffold for situating HARMONiSE's technical contribution inside the academic MSP conversation, rather than explaining it from scratch every time.

Story Notes

A review published in Ocean and Coastal Management surveys geospatial technologies used to improve Marine Spatial Planning, cataloguing the challenges and opportunities across remote sensing, GIS and related tools as applied to MSP processes.

The review complements a separate March 2026 workshop, "Marine Data for MSP," run through the Copernicus Marine Service, which focused on how open ocean data supports maritime spatial planning specifically in practice rather than in the academic literature.

sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0964569122002563
Story Article

Every field builds a shared taxonomy of its own open problems eventually, and Marine Spatial Planning's geospatial technology literature now has one worth reading closely: a review cataloguing the challenges and opportunities of geospatial tools across the MSP process, from data collection through remote sensing to the GIS-based analysis that turns raw spatial data into a usable plan.

A review paper like this does something a single case study cannot. It maps the field's known gaps in one place, cited and structured, which means any new technical contribution, HARMONiSE included, can be positioned explicitly against that taxonomy rather than described in isolation. Reviewers and readers alike trust a contribution more when it names precisely which catalogued challenge it addresses, instead of presenting itself as a standalone innovation with no stated relationship to existing work.

This is a publishing-landscape opportunity as much as a technical one. Citing this review as the framing device for a HARMONiSE paper or a Shorelines piece on the Ocean Mapping Hub's methodology does two things at once: it situates the work inside an active academic conversation rather than describing it from scratch, and it signals to reviewers that the framework was built with awareness of the field's documented gaps, not in isolation from them.

Name the honest limitation directly: a broad review paper like this one is, by design, generic. It surveys geospatial MSP challenges globally, without the SIDS-specific constraints that actually shape what is feasible for a country like Seychelles, thin technical benches, limited survey vessel access, data sovereignty questions layered on top of already sparse coverage. That gap is not a flaw in the review. It is the space a SIDS-focused extension of it would need to fill.

The concrete task is reading the review's stated taxonomy of challenges closely and building a short table mapping each category against what HARMONiSE and the Ocean Mapping Hub already address, partially address, or do not yet touch. That table becomes the backbone of a methods section for a future publication, and a fast way to see exactly where the framework's real technical gaps sit before committing time to closing them.
