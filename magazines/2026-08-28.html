The Seabed Authority Faces Its Own Due-Process Test

You spent years as Seychelles's national focal point to the International Seabed Authority, so watching NORI and TOML force it into a due-process reckoning under a hard three-day deadline is watching an institution you once represented get stress-tested in real time.

Story Notes

On 31 August, three days from now, NORI, TOML and the ISA Secretariat are each due to report to the Seabed Disputes Chamber of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea on how they have complied with the provisional measures the Chamber ordered on 18 July. The order came out of the Chamber's first contentious case: NORI and TOML accused the ISA of denying them due process during a non-compliance inquiry into mining activity the two companies pursued outside the Authority's own framework.

The Chamber sided with the companies on process, not on the underlying facts. It is a narrow ruling. But it is the first time the ISA's dispute settlement machinery has been tested under a court order, and it lands at the exact moment the US is trying to route around the Authority entirely with its own offshore minerals executive order. Two separate challenges to the same institution, three days apart.

site.uit.no/nclos/2026/08/05/the-order-that-changed-more-than-it-granted
Story Article

Three days from now, the International Seabed Authority has to show its work. NORI, TOML and the ISA Secretariat are each due to report to the Seabed Disputes Chamber of the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea by 31 August, laying out how they have complied with provisional measures the Chamber ordered on 18 July. It is a compliance deadline, not a verdict. But it is the first time in the Authority's history that a court has forced it to answer, on the record, for how it treats the companies it regulates.

The case itself is narrower than the headlines suggest. NORI and TOML, both linked to The Metals Company, are contesting an ISA non-compliance inquiry into mining activity the two companies pursued outside the Authority's own regulatory framework. The companies argued the inquiry denied them due process. The Chamber agreed, at least on procedure, and ordered interim measures requiring the ISA to respect their rights while the inquiry runs its course. That is not an exoneration. It is a ruling that the Authority has to follow its own rules when it investigates rule-breaking, which sounds obvious until you remember the ISA has never had a contentious case reach this stage before.

You spent years as Seychelles's national focal point to this Authority. You know how much of its authority rests on member states trusting a body that has almost no enforcement teeth of its own, and that runs largely on consensus among 168 states plus the EU. A due-process ruling against the Secretariat, even a narrow one, tests that trust in a way nothing else has.

The timing compounds it. The US issued an executive order titled "Unleashing America's Offshore Critical Minerals and Resources," asserting a unilateral pathway to deep-sea mining that treats the ISA's jurisdiction over the international seabed as optional. The US never ratified UNCLOS, so it has never been a full ISA member, and it is now acting on that gap. At the ISA's annual session in Kingston, member states launched legal processes to challenge the US move. Governments are still working out what that response actually looks like.

So you have two challenges landing on the same institution within weeks of each other: one from inside the treaty system, testing whether the ISA's own dispute mechanism can hold up under scrutiny; one from outside it, testing whether a non-party can simply act as if the treaty system does not bind it. Both are really the same question. Does the ISA's authority depend on states choosing to respect it, or does it have teeth independent of that choice?

For Seychelles and for SIDS more broadly, that question is not abstract. The ISA administers the seabed beyond national jurisdiction, the Area, held in trust for humankind under UNCLOS. If a non-party can act unilaterally and a due-process challenge from a sponsored contractor can stall the inquiry process, the practical value of that trusteeship for small ocean states erodes on both fronts at once. Commercial mining in the Pacific could begin as early as 2027 regardless of how either dispute resolves. Watch what NORI, TOML and the Secretariat actually file by 31 August. It will tell you more about the ISA's real authority than any communique from Kingston.

The Pacific SIDS Bloc Splits Over Mining

Nauru and the Cook Islands sponsoring exploration while Palau, Fiji and Samoa push for a moratorium is the Pacific SIDS bloc splitting exactly the way you have watched ocean governance coalitions fracture before, and it matters for how Seychelles positions itself in AU and Commonwealth ocean forums.

Story Notes

Commercial deep-sea mining could start in the Pacific as early as 2027, with no internationally agreed regulatory framework in place to govern it. The ISA has spent more than a decade negotiating a mining code and still has not finished it, which means the first commercial operations could run under contractor-specific arrangements rather than settled rules.

The Pacific island states most exposed to this are not unified. Nauru and the Cook Islands sponsor exploration contracts and want mining to proceed. Palau, Fiji and Samoa have pushed publicly for a moratorium. That split matters beyond the Pacific. It is the same fracture line that shows up whenever a bloc of ocean-dependent small states has to weigh near-term revenue against long-term risk, and it undercuts the negotiating leverage SIDS have when they act as a unified voice at the ISA Council or in BBNJ implementation talks.

pina.com.fj/2026/08/25
Story Article

Commercial deep-sea mining in the Pacific could begin as early as 2027. That date matters because the ISA still has not finished the mining code it has been negotiating since 2014. Operations could start under ad hoc, contractor-specific arrangements instead of a settled regulatory framework, which is precisely the scenario the code was meant to prevent.

What makes this a Pacific story rather than a global one is who is exposed. The exploration contracts driving the timeline are sponsored by Nauru and the Cook Islands, two Pacific SIDS that have bet on deep-sea minerals as a genuine economic opportunity. Palau, Fiji and Samoa, also Pacific SIDS, have pushed for a moratorium, arguing the ecological risk to shared ocean space is not worth the uncertain payoff. Both positions come from small island states with limited fiscal room and high exposure to ocean health. Neither side is wrong to weigh it the way it has.

That is the part worth sitting with. This is not a story about SIDS versus mining companies. It is a story about SIDS disagreeing with each other over how to value the same asset, the deep seabed, when some of them sponsor extraction and others depend on the same water for fisheries and coastal protection. You have seen this fracture before, in fisheries subsidy negotiations, in BBNJ area-based management talks, in the run-up to COP finance asks. A bloc that could otherwise negotiate as one voice ends up negotiating as several, and the states with the most capacity to advocate, not the most exposure, end up shaping the outcome.

For Seychelles, this is instructive rather than direct. Seychelles is not a Pacific state and has no seabed mining contracts sponsored in the Area. But Seychelles sits inside the same structural bind as Nauru or Palau: a small state whose ocean footprint vastly exceeds its land footprint, whose government has to weigh blue economy revenue against long-term ocean health, and whose voice in bodies like the ISA Council or AU ocean forums depends on presenting a coherent position rather than a hedged one. As African Union champion for the blue economy, Seychelles has some influence over how African coastal states position themselves on exactly this kind of trade-off. Watching how the Pacific bloc split, and what it cost them at the ISA table, is a preview of a fight Seychelles could face in a different ocean basin.

The other lesson is about timelines outrunning frameworks. A 2027 start date for commercial mining, arriving before the mining code is finished, means the regulatory gap is not hypothetical. It is the operating environment. If Seychelles or the African bloc ever ends up in a comparable position, sponsoring or contesting activity in an under-regulated space, the Pacific split shows what happens when member states let commercial timelines set the pace instead of the other way round. The states that moved early to build a shared position kept some leverage. The states that split lost it, regardless of which side of the mining question they were on.

Washington Tests the Treaty It Never Signed

A non-UNCLOS-party state asserting unilateral rights over the Area is the exact scenario the treaty regime was built to foreclose, and if the US precedent holds it resets what "held in trust for humankind" actually means for every coastal state that never got a seat at that table.

Story Notes

At the ISA's annual session in Kingston this year, member states launched formal legal processes to challenge the US executive order "Unleashing America's Offshore Critical Minerals and Resources." The order asserts a US pathway to deep-sea mineral extraction that treats ISA jurisdiction over the international seabed, the Area, as something the US can act around rather than something it is bound by.

The US has never ratified UNCLOS, so it was never a full ISA member to begin with. What is new is the willingness to act unilaterally rather than simply stay outside the treaty system. The ISA's statement on the order is careful, procedural, and clearly worried. Governments are still deciding what a real response looks like, beyond a legal process that has no enforcement mechanism against a non-party.

isa.org.jm/news/statement-on-the-us-executive-order
Story Article

The Area, the seabed beyond national jurisdiction, is supposed to be held in trust for humankind. That is not rhetorical language. It is the operating principle UNCLOS built the International Seabed Authority around: no state owns the deep seabed, resources extracted from it are meant to benefit all of humanity including states with no coastline, and the ISA exists to administer that trust on everyone's behalf. The US executive order "Unleashing America's Offshore Critical Minerals and Resources" is the first serious test of whether that principle holds when a major power decides it does not want to be bound by it.

The US never ratified UNCLOS. It has observer status at the ISA at best, not membership. That has been true for decades and has mostly been a quiet inconsistency, the kind of gap international lawyers note and diplomats work around. The executive order changes the character of the gap. It does not just keep the US outside the treaty system. It asserts a domestic legal pathway for US companies to extract seabed minerals regardless of what the ISA authorizes, treating the Authority's jurisdiction as advisory rather than binding.

At the ISA's annual session in Kingston, member states responded by launching formal legal processes to challenge the order. What that produces is genuinely unclear. The ISA has no mechanism to compel compliance from a non-party state. Its only real leverage is collective: refusing to recognize minerals extracted outside its framework, coordinating flag-state and port-state pressure, or building diplomatic cost through UN channels. None of that stops a determined unilateral actor quickly. All of it takes exactly the kind of sustained multilateral coordination that a body built on consensus among 168 states plus the EU struggles to produce fast.

This is where it connects to the seabed disputes chamber case running in parallel. NORI, TOML and the ISA Secretariat are due to report on due-process compliance by 31 August, a case testing whether the Authority's own dispute mechanism has real teeth over the contractors it does regulate. The US order tests something adjacent: whether a state that was never inside the tent can simply act as though the tent does not exist. If the second challenge succeeds where the ISA has no leverage at all, the first case starts to matter less. An institution that cannot bind a determined non-party has a weaker claim to binding anyone else either, eventually.

For a state like Seychelles, whose maritime boundary and ocean governance work has always run through UNCLOS and ISA processes in good faith, the stakes are structural rather than immediate. Seychelles has no seabed mining interest at risk this year. But every SIDS government that has invested in multilateral ocean governance, rather than unilateral capacity, is watching to see whether that investment still pays off when a major power decides the rules are optional. If the ISA's legal challenge produces nothing enforceable, the lesson for every coastal state currently building its ocean governance strategy around multilateral trust is a hard one: the trust only holds as long as every party, including the ones who can afford not to, decides to honor it.

28.7% of the Seafloor Is Now Mapped

28.7% of the seafloor mapped is the exact metric HARMONiSE and the Ocean Mapping Hub exist to move, so every percentage point closed this year is a percentage point of the argument you make for why bathymetric capacity in SIDS deserves sustained funding, not a one-off grant.

Story Notes

The Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 Project has released the GEBCO_2026 grid, its annual update to the global bathymetric map. The new figure: 28.7% of the world's ocean floor is now mapped to modern standards, roughly 104 million square kilometres, with almost five million square kilometres added in the past year alone. Mitsuyuki Unno, Executive Director of the Nippon Foundation, announced the update at the IHO Assembly in Monaco.

That growth rate, five million square kilometres a year, means full coverage by 2030 is still a stretch target rather than a near-certainty, even with crowdsourced bathymetry programs accelerating the pace. Two regional events are coming up if you want direct engagement: the Seabed 2030 Symposium in Cartagena, Colombia, 8-9 October, and the Indo-Pacific Ocean Mapping Meeting and Data Sprint in Kuala Lumpur, 20-22 October, with registration open now.

gebco.net/news/release-gebco2026-grid
Story Article

Twenty-eight point seven percent. That is where the world's ocean floor mapping effort stands as of the GEBCO_2026 grid release, up from roughly 25% a year ago, with the Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 Project adding almost five million square kilometres of new bathymetric data in the past twelve months. Mitsuyuki Unno announced the figure at the International Hydrographic Organization's Assembly in Monaco, framing it as steady progress toward the 2030 target of a complete map of the seafloor.

Steady is the right word, and it is also the problem. At roughly five million square kilometres a year, closing the remaining 71% of the ocean floor by 2030 requires either a dramatic acceleration or an honest reset of the timeline. Neither conversation is happening loudly enough. What is happening instead is a genuinely useful expansion of crowdsourced bathymetry, vessels of opportunity contributing depth soundings during ordinary transits, which has done more to move the percentage in the last three years than any single dedicated survey campaign.

This is the metric your work runs on. HARMONiSE and the Ocean Mapping Hub exist because bathymetric coverage in Seychelles's waters, and across Indian Ocean SIDS more broadly, has historically depended on whichever foreign survey vessel happened to pass through, not on sustained domestic capacity. The 28.7% global figure hides enormous regional unevenness. Well-resourced states with existing hydrographic offices and naval survey capacity closed their gaps years ago. States without that infrastructure, most SIDS included, are the reason the global number is not higher, and they are also the states least able to fund the surveys that would fix it.

That is the argument worth making explicitly, and repeatedly, to funders: Seabed 2030's remaining gap is disproportionately a SIDS capacity gap, not a technology gap. The crowdsourced bathymetry approach helps, but it is opportunistic by design, it fills in wherever ships happen to travel, not wherever coverage is most needed for coastal state planning, marine spatial planning, or hazard modelling. A SIDS government cannot build a marine spatial plan, price a blue bond, or negotiate an extended continental shelf claim on opportunistic data. It needs targeted, funded survey capacity, which is a different ask than "help us reach 100% globally."

Two events over the next two months are where that argument gets made in the room rather than on paper. The Seabed 2030 Symposium runs in Cartagena, Colombia, on 8-9 October, and the Indo-Pacific Ocean Mapping Meeting and Data Sprint runs in Kuala Lumpur from 20 to 22 October, with registration open now. Neither is geographically convenient to Seychelles, but the Indo-Pacific event in particular is the closer of the two in framing, since it groups Indian Ocean and Pacific SIDS mapping gaps together. If SHORE or Shorelines has a case to make about the HARMONiSE approach as a model for closing SIDS-specific bathymetric gaps, an Indo-Pacific mapping forum with registration currently open is the room to make it in, not a retrospective piece after the fact.

"Too Small and Too Fragmented to Fund"

"Too small and too fragmented to fund" is the sentence that explains, more precisely than any Seychelles-specific number does, why the High-Income SIDS Paradox exists in the first place.

The global financing system treats small island states as too small and too fragmented to fund efficiently.
Story Notes

A new report from ocean research initiative Back to Blue finds SIDS receive only a fraction of the climate finance they actually need, arguing the global financing system treats small island states as too small and too fragmented to fund efficiently. Despite contributing under 1% of global greenhouse gas emissions collectively, SIDS across the Caribbean, Pacific and Indian Ocean face some of the most severe and immediate climate impacts of any group of states.

A related UNCTAD analysis of 606 ocean-related measures in SIDS national climate plans found 64% of those measures depend on external support to be implemented at all. That figure is the quantified version of a pattern you already argue in the High-Income SIDS Paradox: Seychelles gets classified out of concessional finance eligibility by GNI per capita, while its actual fiscal capacity to fund ocean-related climate adaptation from domestic revenue remains nowhere close to what the classification implies.

unctad.org/news/blog-climate-finance-sids-shockingly-low-why-needs-change
Story Article

"Too small and too fragmented to fund." That is the working diagnosis in a new Back to Blue report on why SIDS receive only a fraction of the climate finance they need, and it is worth sitting with because it is a structural critique, not a funding-level complaint. The claim is not that donors are stingy. It is that the mechanics of the global climate finance system, built around large, bundled project pipelines and transaction costs that make small grants inefficient to process, systematically disadvantage exactly the states with the smallest absorptive capacity and the highest per-capita exposure.

The numbers underneath that claim are stark. SIDS collectively contribute under 1% of global greenhouse gas emissions. They face, disproportionately, the earliest and most severe consequences of a crisis they did almost nothing to cause. A complementary UNCTAD analysis looked at 606 distinct ocean-related measures across SIDS national climate action plans and found 64% of them depend on external support to be implemented. Not improved by external support. Dependent on it. Nearly two-thirds of what SIDS say they need to do on ocean-related climate adaptation cannot happen without financing that the same system this report critiques has consistently failed to deliver at scale.

This is the empirical backbone for the High-Income SIDS Paradox argument, and it deserves to be used that way rather than treated as adjacent context. The paradox, as you have framed it, is that Seychelles gets classified as high-income by GNI per capita, which is a genuinely misleading metric for a small population with an economy concentrated in a handful of vulnerable sectors, and that classification then locks Seychelles out of concessional finance windows designed for lower-income states. The Back to Blue framing gives that argument a mechanism, not just a symptom. It is not only that Seychelles gets classified wrong. It is that the entire system, even for states classified correctly as low-income SIDS, is structurally bad at delivering finance efficiently to small, fragmented recipients. Seychelles's income classification compounds a system that was already failing SIDS as a category before GNI per capita entered the picture.

That distinction matters for how you pitch the paradox to funders and to peers. "Reclassify Seychelles" is a narrower ask than "fix a financing architecture that penalizes smallness and fragmentation regardless of income bracket." The second framing puts Seychelles's specific problem inside a documented, quantified, multilateral-agency-acknowledged failure mode, rather than asking a funder to make a one-off exception for a country that looks rich on paper. It also opens a natural coalition: every SIDS government, high-income-classified or not, has a stake in fixing the mechanism itself, which is a stronger negotiating bloc than Seychelles arguing its case alone.

The next Shorelines piece that draws on the High-Income SIDS Paradox should lead with the 64% figure and the "too small and too fragmented" framing before it gets to Seychelles specifically. Make the systemic case first. Let Seychelles be the illustration, not the whole argument.

A FAIR-data federated standard for marine spatial data is the interoperability layer HARMONiSE eventually has to plug into or compete against, so it is worth understanding the OGC's design choices now rather than after they harden into the default.

STORY NOTES

The Open Geospatial Consortium's Marine Domain Working Group has been developing a Federated Marine Spatial Data Infrastructure, FMSDI, built around FAIR data principles: findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable. The goal is to let marine spatial data infrastructures interoperate across national and institutional boundaries rather than remaining siloed, which is the same interoperability problem SIDS mapping and marine spatial planning programs run into constantly.

Separately, AI-GIS integration is accelerating across marine mapping applications: shoreline detection, coastal erosion monitoring, biodiversity monitoring, and marine spatial planning support, increasingly drawing on remote sensing, autonomous underwater vehicles, and real-time monitoring feeds. The direction of travel is toward federated, interoperable, AI-assisted marine data infrastructure as the emerging default, which is the exact space HARMONiSE occupies.

oceanwise-global.com/guides-and-insights/how-geoai-is-transforming-marine-mapping
STORY ARTICLE

The Open Geospatial Consortium's Marine Domain Working Group has spent the past several years building something worth tracking closely: a Federated Marine Spatial Data Infrastructure, or FMSDI, designed around FAIR data principles, findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable. Strip the acronyms and the pitch is simple. Marine spatial data currently sits in silos, one country's hydrographic office here, a research institute's bathymetry dataset there, a coastal management agency's shoreline layer somewhere else, largely unable to talk to each other without manual reformatting. FMSDI is an attempt to build the plumbing that lets those silos interoperate by default.

That is the exact problem HARMONiSE exists to solve at SIDS scale, and it is worth being precise about the relationship. FMSDI is a standards and interoperability layer, not a delivery mechanism for the capacity gap in the SIDS that most need it. A federated standard is only as useful as the data infrastructures that adopt it, and adoption tends to follow wherever the resourced institutions are already operating: national hydrographic offices, well-funded research consortia, established marine spatial planning bodies in states that already have the survey capacity to generate data worth federating. A SIDS government without a functioning hydrographic office or a domestic bathymetric survey program has nothing to plug into the federation yet, standard or no standard.

This is where HARMONiSE's positioning matters. If the framework is built to be FAIR-compliant and interoperable with whatever standard OGC's Marine Domain Working Group settles on, Seychelles data generated through the Ocean Mapping Hub becomes discoverable and usable by the same infrastructure that well-resourced states are building into by default. If it is not built that way, or if that decision gets made later rather than now while the standard is still forming, HARMONiSE risks producing high-quality data that sits outside the interoperability layer the rest of the field is converging on, which recreates the exact silo problem FMSDI was designed to solve, just at a smaller scale.

The AI layer compounds this. GeoAI applications in marine mapping, shoreline detection from satellite imagery, coastal erosion monitoring, biodiversity pattern recognition from remote sensing feeds, are advancing fast, and nearly all of them are trained and validated against whatever data infrastructure they can access programmatically. A federated, standards-compliant infrastructure is far more useful training and validation ground for these tools than a fragmented one. If SIDS bathymetric and coastal data sits outside the federation, it also sits outside the datasets that the next generation of GeoAI coastal monitoring tools gets trained on, which means those tools will perform worse, by default, on SIDS coastlines than on well-resourced ones. That is a data sovereignty and a data equity problem wearing a technical standards costume.

The OGC's Marine Domain Working Group is a body worth having a seat at, or at minimum a watching brief on, before FMSDI's design choices harden into the industry default. Standards bodies move slowly and then suddenly. Getting SIDS-specific interoperability requirements considered while the standard is still forming costs far less effort than retrofitting them after adoption has already concentrated around well-resourced early movers.

Seychelles Keeps Getting Picked as the Model

The Commonwealth's new strategy to protect Seychelles's 1.4 million square kilometre EEZ is the closest thing on record to an outside institution validating the argument you have been making about Seychelles as a hydrospatial governance case study, and it is happening in your own back yard.

Story Notes

The Commonwealth has launched a partnership with the Government of Seychelles built around a new strategy to protect Seychelles's 1.4 million square kilometre Exclusive Economic Zone while accelerating sustainable ocean-based economic growth. It follows a broader pattern this year: the June 2026 Island States Ocean Summit in Tokyo, a June state visit from India's Prime Minister producing nineteen bilateral outcomes including maritime cooperation, and Seychelles's continued role as African Union champion for the blue economy.

Read together, these are not isolated diplomatic wins. They are Seychelles being repeatedly positioned, by external institutions, as a working model for how a small ocean state governs a disproportionately large maritime territory. That is the exact argument the High-Income SIDS Paradox makes, minus the finance-access critique. The governance credibility is real. The financing mismatch persists alongside it.

thecommonwealth.org/news/roadmap-boost-seychelles-blue-ocean-economy
Story Article

Seychelles governs 1.4 million square kilometres of ocean with a land area you could drive across in an afternoon. That ratio, roughly 3,300 to 1 between maritime and terrestrial territory, is the single fact that makes every other Seychelles ocean governance story make sense, and it is the fact the Commonwealth's newly launched EEZ protection strategy is explicitly built around. The partnership pairs a strategy to protect that EEZ with an explicit growth agenda, framing ocean-based economic activity and ocean protection as the same project rather than a trade-off, which is not how most maritime strategy documents get written.

This did not land in isolation. In June, Seychelles used the Island States Ocean Summit in Tokyo, the first of its kind, to position itself on ocean science, fisheries management, maritime security and ocean finance alongside other large ocean states. Weeks later, India's Prime Minister made a state visit producing nineteen bilateral outcomes, spanning development finance, maritime cooperation, digital payments and institutional capacity-building. Seychelles also continues to hold the African Union's blue economy champion role, using AU meetings to keep ocean priorities inside Agenda 2063 rather than letting them get crowded out by land-based development agendas, which is a real risk given how AU institutional attention typically gets allocated.

Stack these together and a pattern emerges that is worth naming precisely: Seychelles is being repeatedly selected, by the Commonwealth, by India, by the AU, as a demonstration case for small-state ocean governance. Not asked to demonstrate potential. Selected as an existing example other states are meant to learn from. That is a different kind of credibility than aid recipient status, and it is the credibility the High-Income SIDS Paradox argument needs to sit alongside its finance critique, or the argument risks reading as pure grievance rather than a genuine structural mismatch.

Because the mismatch is real and it sits right next to this good news. Seychelles's GNI per capita classification locks it out of concessional finance windows built for lower-income states, even as external institutions treat Seychelles's governance capacity as advanced enough to model for others. Both things are true simultaneously: Seychelles governs its ocean well enough that the Commonwealth, India and the AU all want to partner on strategy and capacity, and Seychelles's fiscal capacity to fund that governance from domestic revenue alone remains nowhere close to what a high-income classification implies. The Blue Bond, Seychelles's now well-cited debt-for-nature and marine finance instrument, exists precisely because conventional concessional finance was not available at the scale the EEZ required. It was an innovation born of exclusion, not choice.

The Commonwealth EEZ strategy is worth reading in full, not just the announcement. If it comes with financing commitments attached, that is a genuinely new data point: an institution treating Seychelles as a governance model and backing that assessment with capital, rather than governance recognition arriving disconnected from the money. If it does not come with financing attached, that gap between recognition and resourcing is itself the cleanest illustration of the paradox this newsletter has been building toward.

BBNJ's Real Work Is Only Starting

BBNJ moving from ratification into an implementation phase, with area-based management tools and marine genetic resource benefit-sharing rules still being written, is the exact moment when your Maritime Boundary Delimitation experience becomes directly relevant to a live negotiating table.

Story Notes

The BBNJ Agreement entered into force on 17 January this year, 120 days after crossing the 60-ratification threshold in September 2025. It now counts 83 parties and 145 signatories, a genuinely fast ratification pace by treaty standards, and it exists to do something UNCLOS never fully addressed: govern biodiversity in the roughly two-thirds of the ocean that sits beyond any state's national jurisdiction, the high seas and the international seabed area combined.

Entry into force is a milestone, not a finish line. The Preparatory Commission's third session, running from 23 March to 2 April this year, worked through the institutional scaffolding, rules of procedure, financial arrangements, the shape of the scientific and technical body, that has to exist before the Conference of the Parties can convene its first meeting. COP1 is where the substantive machinery actually gets defined: how area-based management tools, the treaty's mechanism for designating high-seas marine protected areas, will be proposed and adopted; how environmental impact assessments for activities on the high seas will be conducted and by whom; and critically, how the benefit-sharing regime for marine genetic resources, the provision that determines whether the economic value of high-seas biodiversity gets shared with states that lack the deep-sea research capacity to access it directly, will actually function in practice.

This is the phase where your specific professional background becomes directly relevant rather than generally relevant. Maritime boundary delimitation is a technical discipline built around precisely defined spatial claims, baselines, equidistance principles, the treatment of overlapping and disputed zones. Area-based management tools under BBNJ require exactly that kind of technical precision, applied to a new problem: defining the boundaries of high-seas protected areas in a legal space that, unlike an EEZ, has no coastal state to anchor the claim. Negotiators drafting these tools need people who understand both the legal architecture of maritime boundaries and the practical mechanics of drawing one, and that combination is rarer than it should be at this stage of BBNJ implementation.

There is also a direct SIDS equity angle running underneath the technical one. Marine genetic resources on the high seas have historically been accessed almost exclusively by states and institutions with deep-sea research vessels and genomic sequencing capacity, a capability concentrated in a small number of wealthy states. SIDS, including Seychelles, are net beneficiaries in principle of the benefit-sharing regime BBNJ is meant to establish, but only if that regime gets designed with real teeth rather than becoming a symbolic clause states can point to without funding. The gap between "SIDS benefit in principle" and "SIDS benefit in practice" will be decided in the technical detail of how benefit-sharing gets operationalized at COP1 and the sessions that follow, not in the headline entry-into-force announcement.

If SHORE or Shorelines has a stake in BBNJ implementation, now, in this institutional design phase before COP1, is when input has the most leverage. Once area-based management tools and benefit-sharing mechanics get codified, retrofitting SIDS equity considerations into an established process is a much harder fight than shaping the process while it is still being built.

Story Article

21.1 degrees Celsius. That is the new record for global average sea surface temperature, reported by the EU's Copernicus Climate Change Service, and it is worth holding onto as a specific number rather than folding it into the general and increasingly numbing category of "record ocean heat." A specific figure is falsifiable, comparable across years, and usable as a baseline in a policy document or a Shorelines argument in a way that "hottest on record" alone is not.

The driver is a compounding effect: a still-growing, unusually large natural El Niño event layered on top of the longer-run warming trend from human emissions. Separating the two is genuinely difficult in real time, which is part of why single-year records get contested even when the underlying warming trend is not. What is not contested is the direction. Sea surface temperature records have been broken repeatedly across recent years, and 21.1°C is simply the latest data point in a trend line that has been moving one way for a long time.

The more scientifically interesting part of this story is not the temperature record itself. It is what researchers found when they looked at the ocean carbon sink's response to it. Given the heat, the expected result was a measurable surge in carbon dioxide outgassing from the ocean back into the atmosphere, since warmer water holds less dissolved gas. That surge, so far, has not fully materialized at the scale models predicted. Physical and biological processes, the specific mechanisms are still being worked out, appear to be partially compensating, keeping more carbon in the ocean than a simple temperature-solubility relationship alone would predict.

Researchers are careful, and you should be too, about what that means. It is not evidence the ocean carbon sink is fine. It is evidence that the relationship between ocean warming and carbon release is more complicated than the simplest model, and that the compensating processes currently holding are not guaranteed to continue as warming accumulates further. The honest scientific position is: the sink held better than expected this year, and nobody currently knows how long that holds.

For coastal science and ocean governance work, this distinction matters more than the headline number. A record temperature is a fact you can cite. An open question about carbon sink resilience is a risk that has to be built into planning assumptions, not resolved by citing the good news that outgassing did not spike as predicted. Any SIDS coastal resilience or blue carbon strategy built on the assumption that ocean carbon uptake will continue at historical rates is making a bet on an unresolved scientific question, not a settled one. That is worth stating plainly in any technical document that leans on ocean carbon sink capacity as part of its resilience case, rather than treating the sink's current performance as a stable input.

The practical takeaway for your own writing: cite 21.1°C as the temperature fact. Cite the outgassing finding as an open question, not a reassurance. The two halves of this story get conflated constantly in mainstream coverage, in a way that flattens a genuinely uncertain scientific finding into either alarm or false comfort. Neither is accurate.
