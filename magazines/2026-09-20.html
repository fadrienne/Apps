BBNJ Agreement — Ocean Governance

COP1 Is Set. The Clearing-House Fight Starts Now.

The rulebook for the water outside anyone's jurisdiction just cleared its last drafting stage, and COP1 in January 2027 is where you get a formal seat at deciding how area-based management tools actually get proposed and vetted, which is the exact machinery HARMONiSE needs to plug into.

Story Notes

PrepCom3 wrapped in New York on 2 April 2026, the third and final preparatory session before the BBNJ Agreement's first Conference of the Parties. Delegates locked down rules of procedure, secretariat arrangements, funding channels through the Global Environment Facility, and the operational shape of the Clearing-House Mechanism, then forwarded the whole package to COP1, now scheduled for January 2027.

For you this is not abstract diplomacy. Whoever shapes its early data standards shapes what counts as usable evidence, and that is a hydrospatial data question before it is a legal one.

COP1 for the BBNJ Agreement is now fixed for January 2027, and the path there closed on 2 April 2026 when PrepCom3 finished its two-week session at UN Headquarters. This was the third and final preparatory meeting under the Agreement, and its job was mechanical rather than dramatic: lock down the rules of procedure, agree how the secretariat will function, settle the Agreement's relationship with the Global Environment Facility, and decide the operational shape of the Clearing-House Mechanism before handing all of it to the first Conference of the Parties for adoption.

None of that sounds like it touches your work. It does, directly, because the Clearing-House Mechanism is the piece of BBNJ infrastructure that decides what data counts. Area-based management tools, the marine protected areas and other spatial designations the Agreement exists to create in the high seas, get proposed and reviewed through that mechanism. So do environmental impact assessments for activities beyond national jurisdiction. Both processes need bathymetric, habitat and species distribution data to run on, and the mechanism is still deciding what formats, resolutions and metadata standards it will accept.

That is a hydrospatial data governance question, not a legal one, and it is being settled now, before COP1 rather than after. If the Clearing-House Mechanism defaults to the data standards and submission formats that well-funded Global North institutions already use, coastal states without that infrastructure, including most of the Western Indian Ocean, start the BBNJ era at a structural disadvantage. Seychelles ratified the Agreement early, which gives it and organisations like SHORE a credible platform to argue for standards that accommodate the mapping capacity SIDS states actually have, rather than the capacity a handful of large maritime nations have.

There is a second thread worth tracking. Marine genetic resources and benefit-sharing, the part of BBNJ that drew the most negotiating attention for years, still needs its monetary and non-monetary benefit-sharing mechanisms worked out in practice, not just on paper. The Preparatory Commission's work on this stayed largely procedural through PrepCom3, which means the substantive fight over how SIDS states actually capture value from marine genetic resource research done in their waters, or near them, is still ahead. That is a science-to-policy pipeline problem, and it is one Shorelines readers who work in ocean governance will want a clear explainer on before COP1, not after the fact.

For SHORE specifically, the practical move is to get a position, even an informal one, into the record before January 2027. PrepCom3's summary report is public and gives the shape of what COP1 will be asked to adopt. Reading it now, and drafting a short technical note on Clearing-House Mechanism data standards for SIDS hydrospatial capacity, is a concrete Shorelines piece that positions you ahead of the COP rather than reacting to it after the fact. The window to influence early practice is between now and January, not after.

Global ocean floor mapping now stands at 28.7 percent, the Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 Project confirmed in its latest annual update. That is roughly 104 million square kilometres of seafloor with usable bathymetric data. The regional breakdown is the part worth sitting with. The ROPME Sea Area, covering the Gulf, jumped from about 6.4 percent coverage to 20.5 percent in a single year, more than tripling. That did not happen by accident. It happened because regional states and Seabed 2030's Regional Centre structure targeted that basin specifically, pooled contributions, and processed them into the grid on a defined timeline. It is proof that a coordinated regional push, not just incidental crowdsourced data, is what moves the coverage number.

Either way, that is a gap SHORE is positioned to name precisely, using the same GEBCO grid data that is free and public, rather than arguing from a general sense that the region lacks coverage.

This is where the Seabed 2030 milestone turns into a funding argument rather than a status update. Building that same case for the Western Indian Ocean, anchored in GEBCO's own published coverage statistics rather than estimates, is a stronger pitch to IOC-UNESCO, the Nippon Foundation, or bilateral partners than a general ocean mapping capacity appeal.

There is also a direct Ocean Mapping Hub angle. Every crowdsourced bathymetry contribution submitted through Seabed 2030's four Regional Centres becomes part of the next grid release, which means the Hub's own data, if formatted and submitted correctly, has a direct and traceable path into a dataset used globally for maritime boundary work, habitat modelling, and tsunami risk assessment. That pipeline is worth documenting properly in a Shorelines piece: not just that the Hub collects data, but exactly how a single survey line becomes part of the international bathymetric record other researchers and policymakers will cite for years.

ISA / NOAA — Governance Flashpoint

The Multilateral Seabed Regime Just Failed Its First Real Stress Test

While the International Seabed Authority stalls on exploitation rules, the United States just approved the first-ever unilateral deep-sea mining license outside that framework entirely, and that precedent is the single biggest threat to the multilateral seabed governance model HARMONiSE assumes will hold.

Story Notes

The ISA Council's March 2026 session in Kingston, Jamaica ended without finalising the Draft Exploitation Regulations, despite over a decade of negotiation, and pushed intersessional work through to a second session later in the year. Meanwhile, under an April 2025 executive order, NOAA amended the US Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act and in March 2026 found that TMC USA's consolidated application, the first under the streamlined domestic process, was in substantial compliance with statutory requirements, for both an exploration and a commercial recovery license.

This is two competing governance tracks running in parallel: a multilateral one, the ISA, still negotiating the rules it needs before it can legally authorise commercial mining, and a unilateral one, the US, acting under domestic law outside a treaty it never ratified, moving toward commercial licensing without waiting for the ISA to finish.

For anyone working in maritime boundary or ocean governance policy, this is the live test case for whether the multilateral seabed regime holds when a major power decides not to wait for it. The outcome shapes precedent for every other unilateral claim to areas beyond national jurisdiction, not just seabed minerals.

Story Article

Two seabed governance systems are now operating on the same physical seafloor, and they disagree about who is in charge. The International Seabed Authority, the multilateral body created under UNCLOS to regulate mineral extraction in international waters, ended its March 2026 Council session in Kingston without finalising the Draft Exploitation Regulations it has been negotiating for more than a decade. The Council pushed intersessional work through the following months and scheduled a further session later in the year, still without a finished rulebook.

The United States, which never ratified UNCLOS and is not bound by the ISA's authority, moved in the opposite direction. Under an executive order signed in April 2025, NOAA amended the domestic Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act to create a consolidated application process, allowing a single filing to cover both an exploration license and a commercial recovery license. In March 2026, NOAA determined that the first application filed under this streamlined process, submitted by TMC USA LLC, was in substantial compliance with US statutory requirements. That is a procedural finding, not a final approval, but it moves a private company closer to commercial deep-sea mining outside the ISA's framework than any actor has come before.

The significance is not really about minerals. It is about which governance model prevails when a major power decides multilateral process is too slow. The ISA's decade-plus delay on exploitation rules is a real failure of institutional pace, and it is the exact opening a state acting under domestic law needs to argue that unilateral action is reasonable when the multilateral body cannot finish its own rulebook. If that argument holds for seabed minerals, it holds, in principle, for other activities in areas beyond national jurisdiction that a state decides it does not want to wait on a treaty body to authorise.

That is precisely the environment BBNJ enters as it heads toward COP1 in January 2027. BBNJ assumes states will route new activity in the high seas, marine protected area proposals, environmental impact assessments, benefit-sharing from marine genetic resources, through its own multilateral machinery. A precedent where the world's largest economy instead licenses seabed activity unilaterally, justified partly by the multilateral body's own institutional delay, is a direct challenge to that assumption, whether or not the two regimes ever formally collide in the same waters.

For SIDS states and institutes like SHORE that have staked real capacity-building effort on the multilateral model, the practical question is not whether this precedent is legally sound, that fight will run in courts and diplomatic channels for years, but whether it changes the incentive calculus for other states watching. If unilateral seabed licensing proceeds without meaningful consequence, other states with the capacity to act outside multilateral frameworks may conclude the same approach works elsewhere in ocean governance. Tracking how the ISA's next intersessional session and the TMC USA license proceed together, rather than as separate stories, is the clearest signal available right now for whether the multilateral seabed regime survives its first real unilateral stress test.

Copernicus Marine / SDB GUI — Hydrospatial Tools

Two Cost Barriers to Satellite Bathymetry Just Disappeared

Copernicus Marine just made global 100-metre-resolution coastal bathymetry free and public, and that is exactly the kind of open dataset the Ocean Mapping Hub should be citing as baseline data instead of commissioning from scratch.

Story Notes

Copernicus Marine Service expanded its coastal data catalogue with new global satellite-derived bathymetry products, delivering 100-metre-resolution depth information for coastal waters, built from satellite imagery rather than in-situ survey. Separately, an open-source SDB GUI tool, version 4.1.1, was published in March 2026, a free Python-based interface for processing satellite-derived bathymetry without commercial software licensing.

Satellite-derived bathymetry has a real limitation set, coastal and shallow-water accuracy depends on water clarity and depth, and it will not replace multibeam survey for navigational-grade charting. But for baseline regional coverage in a data-poor coastal zone, it closes gaps that would otherwise sit unmapped indefinitely.

The combination, free global coastal data plus a free open-source processing tool, removes both cost barriers that typically keep this kind of capability out of a small institute's reach. That is worth testing directly against Seychelles's own coastal waters as a Hub proof of concept.

Story Article

Two releases, six months apart, have quietly removed the two biggest cost barriers to running satellite-derived bathymetry work out of a small institute. Copernicus Marine Service, the EU's operational ocean monitoring programme, expanded its catalogue with global coastal satellite-derived bathymetry products at 100-metre resolution, free and public, built from optical satellite imagery rather than ship-based survey. And an open-source processing tool, SDB GUI, reached version 4.1.1 in March 2026, a Python-based interface that handles the machine-learning pipeline satellite-derived bathymetry needs, including integrated ICESat-2 laser altimetry retrieval, without requiring a commercial GIS licence to run it.

Satellite-derived bathymetry works by inferring depth from how light attenuates through water in different spectral bands, calibrated against reference depth points. It is not a replacement for multibeam sonar survey, and its accuracy degrades with water turbidity and depth, generally useful to somewhere between 15 and 25 metres in clear water, less in anything murkier. Navigational charting still needs ship-based survey. But for a coastal state trying to establish baseline bathymetric coverage across a large EEZ with limited survey vessel time, satellite-derived bathymetry is the only method that can plausibly cover the whole coastline in a single pass, repeated over time to track change.

That is the specific gap the Ocean Mapping Hub sits in front of. Seychelles's EEZ is large relative to its survey capacity, and any coastal or nearshore area that has not had a dedicated multibeam campaign run against it is effectively unmapped for planning purposes. Global 100-metre coastal data from Copernicus, cross-referenced against whatever in-situ survey points SHORE already holds for calibration, gives a first-pass baseline for exactly those unmapped stretches, at zero data acquisition cost. The processing tool removes the second constraint, the need for a licensed remote sensing suite or in-house machine learning pipeline to turn the raw imagery into a usable depth grid.

The honest caveat belongs in any Shorelines piece on this: satellite-derived bathymetry baseline data is a starting point, not a finished product, and any output built from it should be labelled by its actual accuracy tier rather than presented as survey-grade. The value is in coverage, not precision. Where it earns its place is habitat modelling, first-pass hazard screening, and identifying which specific coastal stretches most urgently need a proper multibeam campaign, turning a vague need for more mapping capacity into a data-backed prioritisation of where the next survey budget should actually go.

The practical next step is a direct test: pull the Copernicus coastal tile for a stretch of Seychelles's coastline SHORE already has calibration points for, run it through SDB GUI, and compare the output against known depths. If the accuracy holds at the resolution Copernicus claims, that becomes a concrete, citable proof of concept for a Hub capability that currently exists only as a plan.

Story Article

A short administrative notification is quietly one of the more consequential BBNJ documents to land this year. Notification 2026-001, issued by the UN Division for Ocean Affairs and the Law of the Sea, invites every Party to the BBNJ Agreement to designate a national focal point, the individual or office responsible for sending and receiving official notifications under the Agreement and acting as the country's liaison with the secretariat.

On paper this reads like routine diplomatic housekeeping, the kind of procedural step that accompanies any treaty entering into force. In practice, the focal point role sits directly upstream of decisions that matter far more than the appointment itself suggests. The Clearing-House Mechanism, the digital and administrative infrastructure through which BBNJ's substantive work happens, area-based management tool proposals, environmental impact assessment information, marine genetic resource notifications, routes through national focal points, particularly during this pre-COP1 period while the mechanism's formal procedures are still being finalised. Whoever holds that role for Seychelles is, in effect, the first filter on what national ocean data reaches the international process and how it is framed when it does.

This matters specifically because the Clearing-House Mechanism's data standards are not yet fixed. Research published in Frontiers in Ocean Sustainability has already raised the question of how the mechanism should handle data-centric capacity development for Parties without extensive existing data infrastructure, precisely the situation most SIDS states are in relative to major maritime nations. A national focal point who understands hydrospatial data formats, and who is positioned to advocate for the country's actual data capacity rather than a generic administrative process, changes what Seychelles is able to contribute and access through the mechanism from day one.

For SHORE, this is not a story to read passively. If Seychelles's government has not yet finalised who will hold the national focal point role, or if the decision is being made without technical input on what the role actually requires once the Clearing-House Mechanism goes live, that is a gap worth closing now. A focal point without hydrospatial data background may default to whatever submission format the mechanism suggests, rather than pushing for one that fits the kind of survey and habitat data SHORE and Seychelles's marine spatial planning process actually generate.

The window here is short and specific. Notification 2026-001 does not set a hard deadline in what has been published, but PrepCom3's conclusion and the run-up to COP1 in January 2027 mean early-appointed focal points will be the ones shaping how the mechanism actually operates in its first working months, before later appointees are even in position. Raising this directly with the ministry responsible for BBNJ implementation, ideally with a specific case for why the focal point role needs hydrospatial data fluency, is a concrete, low-cost intervention with a real chance of shaping outcomes rather than reacting to them a year from now.

The Ocean Needs $175 Billion a Year. Seychelles Can't Access Most of It.

This is the High-Income SIDS Paradox in a single data point. Story Article

$175 billion a year. That is the financing gap UNCTAD's 5th UN Ocean Forum put back on the table for what sustainable ocean sectors actually need annually to reach SDG 14, and the forum's own framing called for a blue deal, an urgent scale-up of both public and private investment, to close it. The number itself is not new. What is worth pulling apart is who is positioned to access that financing when it moves, and who is not, because that split is the High-Income SIDS Paradox made concrete rather than argued in the abstract.

Multilateral climate and blue economy finance is still largely gated by income classification. The World Bank's GNI-per-capita thresholds determine eligibility for a wide range of concessional windows, and Seychelles, by that measure, sits in the high-income bracket. That classification exists for good statistical reasons at a global level. It also produces a specific distortion for small island states: GDP per capita measured at the national level says nothing about physical exposure to sea-level rise, storm intensity, or the structural cost of building climate-resilient infrastructure across a widely dispersed archipelago. A country can be classified high-income and still face a blue economy financing need that is, in absolute structural terms, closer to its lower-income SIDS peers than to any continental high-income economy.

This is precisely the gap a $175 billion annual financing shortfall makes visible. If the total need is that large, and concessional windows are the primary channel through which SIDS states typically access blue economy and climate finance, then income-classification exclusions do not just affect a handful of edge-case countries. They remove entire categories of small island states, Seychelles included, from a meaningful share of whatever new capital a blue deal is meant to mobilise, at the exact moment the total pool is being framed as urgently insufficient.

The argument that vulnerability, not income, should gate blue economy finance eligibility is not new either. What has been missing from that argument, consistently, is a large, current, credibly sourced figure to anchor it against. A $175 billion annual gap, cited at a UN-convened forum in 2026, is exactly that anchor. It turns a claim that Seychelles is unfairly excluded from concessional finance into a claim that Seychelles is excluded from access to a share of a documented $175 billion annual shortfall that vulnerability-based criteria would meaningfully close, which is a different and much stronger case to make in a policy brief, a funding pitch, or a Shorelines piece aimed at donor or multilateral audiences.

The next Shorelines piece on the High-Income SIDS Paradox should use this figure directly, alongside Seychelles's own blue bond and blue economy GDP contribution data, to build the quantitative case rather than the qualitative one. Naming the specific concessional windows Seychelles is excluded from by income classification, and estimating what share of the $175 billion gap vulnerability-based criteria would actually unlock for high-income SIDS states, converts an equity argument that is easy to nod along to into one that is harder to dismiss with a generic reference to Seychelles's GDP per capita.

Publishing Is Splitting Into Two Price Tiers, and You Should Pick Now

Where you eventually publish your graduate research just got more expensive by design, since Elsevier's Journal of Ocean Engineering and Science is converting fully to gold open access with an article processing charge attached, and that shift is happening across ocean journals faster than the funding to cover it.

Story Notes

The Journal of Ocean Engineering and Science becomes a fully gold open access title from 1 January 2026, joining Elsevier's roster of over 890 fully open access journals. Authors submitting from 1 November 2025 onward will be charged an article processing charge after peer review and acceptance; submissions received earlier are exempt.

This is one journal in a broader shift. Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans already charges close to $4,190 for optional open access. Some smaller society-run journals are moving the opposite direction, the Oceanographic Society of Japan dropped its manuscript listing fee entirely in January 2026 specifically to lower participation barriers.

For someone weighing where to publish graduate research on a SIDS researcher's budget, the practical effect is a widening split between commercial gold-OA journals charging thousands per article and smaller open, no-fee venues, and that split is worth mapping deliberately before choosing a target journal, not discovering after a manuscript is drafted.

Story Article

Publishing in ocean science is quietly sorting itself into two different economic models, and which side of that split you land on will shape where graduate research work can realistically go. The Journal of Ocean Engineering and Science converts to fully gold open access from 1 January 2026, part of Elsevier's push past 890 fully open access titles. Every article submitted from 1 November 2025 onward is charged an article processing charge once it clears peer review, a cost that did not previously exist for that journal and now sits between authors and publication regardless of institutional backing.

This is not an isolated policy change. Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans, one of the field's most cited venues, already charges close to $4,190 for its own open access option. These are not small numbers against a graduate stipend or an early-career researcher's discretionary budget, and they compound the moment a project has co-authors, multiple papers, or supplementary data publications attached to a single piece of research.

Set against that, a smaller, quieter shift is moving the opposite direction. The Oceanographic Society of Japan eliminated its manuscript listing fee entirely in January 2026, regardless of membership status, explicitly to lower the barrier to publishing for authors without institutional funding behind them. The European Geosciences Union's full journal portfolio remains open access without the same fee structure commercial publishers are converting toward. These venues exist, they are credible, and they are being drowned out in general conversation by the scale of the commercial shift happening around them.

The practical implication is that where research should go is no longer a question answerable by journal prestige and topic fit alone. It now has a real cost dimension that needs mapping before a manuscript is drafted, not discovered after submission when an invoice for several thousand dollars arrives with an acceptance letter. For graduate research undertaken without a large institutional grant behind it, the fully gold open access commercial journals are increasingly the expensive default, while the fee-free or lower-fee venues require deliberately seeking out rather than falling into by habit.

There is a second-order point worth raising in a Shorelines piece on this, beyond the personal publishing-strategy angle. If commercial gold open access becomes the field's dominant model, and article processing charges sit in the thousands of dollars, that is a structural barrier to publication for researchers based in SIDS institutions generally, not just an individual budgeting problem. A researcher at a well-funded Global North university absorbs a large article processing charge as a normal grant line item. A SIDS-based researcher, or an independent institute like SHORE, does not have that grant infrastructure as a default. Naming that gap explicitly, with the actual fee figures attached, is a stronger contribution to the open access equity conversation than a general complaint about publishing costs, and it is worth writing before choosing where the next SHORE research output goes.

You Missed the Rio 2027 Door. Don't Miss the Next One.

The 2027 Ocean Decade Conference in Rio is where the Decade's next phase gets set, and even though the Special Events call already closed, the pattern behind it is the thing worth acting on now so the next window doesn't close unnoticed too.

The 2027 Ocean Decade Conference runs 7 to 9 April 2027 in Rio de Janeiro, hosted by the Brazilian government and co-organised with IOC-UNESCO, expected to draw around 3,000 participants, preceded by two days of Special Events on 5 and 6 April dedicated to individual Ocean Decade Solutions or specific ocean regions.

The call for institutions to lead those Special Events closed 31 August 2026, so that specific door is shut. What is still open is everything downstream: the main conference programme, side events, and the broader positioning window as the Ocean Decade heads into its back half toward 2030.

A Western Indian Ocean or SIDS-focused Special Event evidently was not on SHORE's radar in time this round. Worth flagging now, plainly, so the same window does not close unnoticed again when the next Ocean Decade milestone event opens its own call.

Story Article

Rio de Janeiro hosts the 2027 Ocean Decade Conference from 7 to 9 April, co-organised by the Brazilian government and IOC-UNESCO, with roughly 3,000 participants expected. It is preceded by two days of dedicated Special Events on 5 and 6 April, each built around one of the UN Ocean Decade's eight defined Solutions or a specific ocean region, running as full-day programmes ahead of the main conference.

The call for institutions to lead those Special Events closed on 31 August 2026. That window is shut, and there is no value in treating it as still open. What is worth doing instead is naming clearly why it matters that it passed without a Western Indian Ocean or SIDS-focused proposal from SHORE or an aligned partner institution going in, and making sure the next comparable window does not close the same way.

The Ocean Decade Solutions framework groups the Decade's priorities into named categories, spanning ocean mapping, biodiversity conservation, pollution, sustainable food, climate, resilience, technology and knowledge, and capacity development. A Special Event dedicated to any one of these, framed specifically around Western Indian Ocean or SIDS hydrospatial equity, would have put SHORE's core work directly in front of the roughly 3,000 people the main conference is expected to draw, a scale of visibility that is difficult to replicate through a Shorelines post or a single conference panel slot elsewhere.

This is not a story with an immediate action attached to it, since the specific door named in this update is closed. It is a story worth logging precisely because it establishes a pattern: major Ocean Decade milestone events run on calls with lead times of six to eight months, publicised through channels like IOCARIBE, IOC-UNESCO's own news feed, and partner networks like GEO Blue Planet, rather than through a single centralised announcement. Missing the Rio 2027 Special Events call is a reasonable one-off. Missing the equivalent call for whatever comes after Rio, or for regional Ocean Decade Actions calls that open on a rolling basis throughout the year, would not be.

The practical takeaway is procedural rather than urgent: set a standing watch on IOC-UNESCO, IOCARIBE, and Ocean Decade's own news channel for the next call of this kind, and treat any future special event, decade action, or flagship programme call as a deadline to calendar the moment it appears, not something to notice after the fact once the window has already closed, as happened this time.
